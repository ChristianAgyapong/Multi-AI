Photosynthesis: Study Notes

Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants, algae, and some
bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. It
takes place mainly in the chloroplasts of plant cells, specifically using
a green pigment called chlorophyll that absorbs light, primarily in the
red and blue wavelengths, while reflecting green light (which is why
plants appear green).

The overall reaction can be summarized as:
6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy -> C6H12O6 + 6 O2

Photosynthesis happens in two main stages:

1. Light-dependent reactions (in the thylakoid membrane): Light energy is
   absorbed by chlorophyll and used to split water molecules (photolysis),
   releasing oxygen as a byproduct. This stage produces ATP and NADPH,
   which are energy-carrying molecules used in the next stage.

2. Light-independent reactions / Calvin Cycle (in the stroma): ATP and
   NADPH from the first stage are used to convert carbon dioxide into
   glucose. This stage does not require light directly, but depends on the
   products of the light-dependent reactions.

Common student mistakes:
- Confusing photosynthesis with respiration (respiration is the reverse
  process: it consumes glucose and oxygen, and releases CO2 and water,
  releasing energy for the cell rather than storing it).
- Forgetting that oxygen released in photosynthesis comes from splitting
  water, not from carbon dioxide.
- Assuming the Calvin Cycle doesn't happen in the dark — it can happen
  without light, but it depends on ATP/NADPH which are only produced when
  light is available, so in practice both stages run during daylight in
  most plants.

Factors affecting the rate of photosynthesis: light intensity, carbon
dioxide concentration, temperature, and water availability. Rate increases
with more of the first three (up to a point, after which enzymes involved
denature or another factor becomes limiting), and drops sharply if the
plant lacks water.
